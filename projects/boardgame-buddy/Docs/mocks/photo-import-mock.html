Step 1 — the pick five photos, one evening and a convention

Photographs are drawn, not photographed — what is being reviewed here is the metadata, not the picture.

Reading the file

domain/exif.js · ~150 lines

Walks the JPEG segment chain to the APP1/Exif block rather than searching for the string — those four bytes turn up inside compressed image data, and a false hit is a TIFF header pointing anywhere. Six tags are read and the rest ignored, which is why this is a file and not a 30–80 KB library.

The date is taken verbatim, never through a Date. An EXIF timestamp is already local wall-clock time where the shutter fired. Reparsing it in the phone's current zone is what moves a 9pm holiday play to the following day.

The country raster

domain/geo-grid.js + generated data · 162 KB, lazy

A 0.1° grid of the world's borders, run-length encoded as <count base36><ISO code> — self-delimiting with no separators, because run lengths are lower case and country codes are upper case. Coordinate in, country out, in an array index.

The coordinate never leaves the device, and it is never written to the draft. It also never reaches the photo bucket: the copy that uploads is re-encoded through a canvas, which is what strips the tag the app just read.

What gets written

POST /plays/photo, then POST /plays/import

No new endpoint and no migration. PlayCreate already carried photo_url and country_code, and bgb_log_play already wrote both. Photos go up first, one at a time, each URL memoised on its shot; then the plays go in chunks of fifty.

A client_key per play means a half-finished run resumes and re-writes nothing. One import_batch_id for the run means Settings can undo the whole thing in one tap.

Where this is wrong, and how you find out. Country granularity is the ceiling — the play row holds nothing finer — and 0.1° is about 7 km across at 50°N, so a play a few kilometres from a border can land on the wrong side of it. Basel, Geneva and Maastricht are the cities that decided the resolution; Tijuana and Windsor ON still come out wrong, because they are metres from one. That is why the resolved country is on screen on every photo, with the picker one tap away: a guess you can see beats a value you can't.

One photo is one play. Not a session, not an evening. Four shots of one game means removing three, which is one tap each on the pick step — the alternative, grouping by time and place and asking you to un-group, guesses at the thing you came here to say.
